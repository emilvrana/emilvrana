The MVP Trap in AI Projects

July 2026 · Emil Vrána

Here's a pattern I keep seeing: a team wants to "add AI" to their product. They spin up a vector database. They set up a RAG pipeline. They build a retrieval layer, a ranking layer, a guardrail layer, an observability dashboard. Three sprints in, they haven't yet tested whether the model can actually do the thing they hired it for.

This is the MVP trap — but inverted. In software, the MVP strips everything down to test the core hypothesis. In AI projects, teams do the opposite: they build the scaffolding before checking if the building stands.

The real MVP is one prompt

Before you architect anything, answer one question: can the model produce a useful output for a real input?

Not a toy example. Not a cherry-picked demo. A real input — the kind your users will actually send, messy edge cases and all.

Open a notebook. Paste the input. Write a prompt. Look at the output. If it's wrong, tweak the prompt. If it's still wrong after three honest attempts, the model can't do this task — and no amount of pipeline engineering will fix that.

Why this matters

Model capability is the load-bearing wall. Everything else — RAG, orchestration, guardrails — is interior decoration. If the model can't reliably transform your real inputs into acceptable outputs, the project doesn't work. You can't pipeline your way out of a capability gap.

I've seen teams spend six weeks on infrastructure for a task the model gets right 40% of the time. That's not a pipeline problem. That's a "wrong tool for the job" problem.

A simple protocol

  1. Write five real inputs. Not synthetic. Not idealized. The ugliest, longest, most ambiguous things your users will send.
  2. Prompt the model directly. No framework. No chain. Just the model, the input, and a clear instruction.
  3. Score the outputs honestly. Not "with a small fix this would work." Either it works or it doesn't.
  4. If ≥4/5 pass → proceed to infrastructure. If not → you have a research problem, not an engineering problem.

The uncomfortable truth

Sometimes the answer is "the model isn't good enough yet." That's a fine answer. It's better than discovering it after you've built the pipeline.

The model landscape moves fast. What doesn't work today might work in three months. But you won't know unless you tested it honestly in the first place — and you won't remember what you tested unless you wrote it down.

So write the prompt. Try the ugly inputs. Be brutal in your scoring. Then build.

The best AI architecture is the one you build after you know the model works. Not before.
