The Specification Gap

July 1, 2026 · Emil Vrána

You know what you want. You tell the agent what you want. These are not the same thing.

The specification gap is the distance between your intent and your instruction. It's where most agent failures actually live — not in the model, not in the tools, but in the translation from thought to prompt.

The illusion of clarity

"I need a summary of the Q3 report" feels specific. It's not. What length? For what audience? Which sections matter? Should it highlight risks or opportunities? A human colleague would ask. An agent guesses.

And guesses wrong, consistently, in ways that feel random but aren't — they're systematic. The model optimizes for what it thinks you mean, which is an interpolation of every similar request it's seen. That's not your request. That's the average of everyone's request.

Where the gap widens

The gap gets worse with complexity, but not linearly. It gets worse in two specific dimensions:

Ambiguity compounds. Each underspecified instruction doesn't add uncertainty — it multiplies it. A task with three ambiguous steps doesn't have three possible interpretations. It has the product of them. This is why agents that handle single steps well fall apart in pipelines.

Tacit knowledge is invisible to you. You don't write "sort by recency" because it's obvious. You don't specify "exclude draft documents" because of course. You don't say "use the same currency as the input" because who wouldn't? The agent, that's who. Every assumption you didn't write down is a failure mode you didn't anticipate.

Closing it

You can't eliminate the specification gap. You can narrow it.

Make the output contract explicit before the task. Not "summarize" but "produce a 200-word summary for executives, highlighting financial risks, in bullet format." The contract is the real specification. The prompt is just one way to express it.

Test with the adversarial cases. Your specification works for the happy path? Great. Now: what happens with edge inputs? Empty data? Conflicting instructions? If you haven't tested the boundaries, your spec is aspirational.

Iterate on the spec, not the prompt. When the agent fails, the instinct is to tweak the prompt. The discipline is to ask: what did I actually want? Then write that down. The prompt is ephemeral. The specification is the asset.

The uncomfortable truth

Most "agent failures" aren't agent failures. They're specification failures that became visible when the agent executed them faithfully. The agent did what you said. It just didn't do what you meant.

If your agent keeps misunderstanding you, the problem might not be the agent.
