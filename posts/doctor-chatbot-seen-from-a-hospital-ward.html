Doctor Chatbot, Seen From a Hospital Ward

February 12, 2026

My sister Lenka is a nurse on an internal medicine ward, and her patients increasingly arrive with a diagnosis from a chatbot. At my nephew's birthday last month she told me about one who brought a printed conversation and argued with the doctor for twenty minutes. It is not unusual any more, she said; it happens every week. I defended the tools with my usual line: the models pass medical exams, the problem is how people use them. She asked who checks it. We left it there, which in our family counts as a draw.

This week a study in Nature Medicine, led by researchers at Oxford, put both of us to the test. Almost 1,300 people in Britain received one of ten medical situations written by doctors and had to say what the condition might be and what to do about it, from staying at home to calling an ambulance. Some used one of three chatbots, the rest whatever they normally use, mostly a search engine or their own judgment. On their own, the models answered well. The people using them did no better than the people without them, and were worse at naming the right condition.

The reasons are the interesting part, and none of them is about medical knowledge. People did not know what the model needed to hear, so they left out the detail that mattered. The answers mixed good advice with bad, and people picked the part they liked. Small changes in wording changed the advice: two participants describing almost the same symptoms of bleeding in the brain got opposite answers, one told to lie down in a dark room, the other to get emergency care.

I know this result from my own work, far away from medicine. A tool tested on its own and the same tool in the hands of the person who will use it are two different tools, and only the second one exists in real life. In January OpenAI launched ChatGPT Health, a separate section for health questions, but not in Europe, where the rules for health data are stricter. Lenka's patients do not wait for it. They use the ordinary chatbot.

My take: Lenka was right about the result and I was right that the model was not the weak point, which leaves her patients exactly where they were. I sent her the study. She answered with one word: «Vidíš.»
