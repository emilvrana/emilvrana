An Hour in a Staff Room: What Teachers Asked Me About AI

August 28, 2026

On Thursday I gave the hardest talk of my year, to about twenty teachers at a Prague gymnasium, in the week before the school year when the teachers are back and the pupils are not. My friend Ondra, who teaches physics there and sat next to me at school in Šumperk, asked me to explain what pupils actually do with AI and what a teacher can do about it. The schools have moved fast: figures from the National Pedagogical Institute, quoted in the press this month, say that 91% of schools gave their teachers some kind of support with AI in the last school year, up from 62% a year earlier.

I started with a problem from Ondra's own physics homework. The chatbot got the right number with a wrong step in the middle, and wrote the wrong step with exactly the same confidence as the right ones. Nobody in the room needed that explained; they spend their working lives finding the wrong step. The questions that followed were better than most I get from clients. Can I tell whether a pupil used it? Not reliably, and the detectors that promise it are not evidence I would want to show a parent; ask for the steps out loud instead. Can I use it to prepare tests? Yes, and check every answer before a pupil sees it. Can I paste in pupils' work? Not with their names in it.

Then came the experiment that went better than planned. One of the Czech-language teachers asked the model to write a short essay and then, in a fresh conversation, asked the same model to grade it. It gave the essay a one, the top mark in Czech schools, with a paragraph of praise for its «mature style». The room laughed for a long time. It was the clearest demonstration of the afternoon of why a model should not be the only judge of its own work, and I had not planned it.

I left with a great deal of respect for teachers. They have been dealing with this since the first chatbot wrote the first homework essay, mostly on their own, and mostly by changing how they set tasks rather than by banning anything. I learned more from their questions than they did from my slides.

The best hour of my August, in front of twenty people who grade answers for a living.
