Three Out of Six: I Failed an AI Music Blind Test

October 27, 2026

This month the Czech Association for Artificial Intelligence is once again running Měsíc AI, a month of free events at its AI hub in Karlín, ten minutes' walk from where I live now. I have been there on three evenings. The best was a hands-on workshop, and the best part of that evening happened afterwards at the bar, when Veronika, an engineer at a Brno startup who tunes language models so they understand Czech properly, decided to test my ears.

She played six tracks from her phone, and I had to say which were made by people and which by a model. I got three wrong, which is exactly what a coin would have managed. The worst miss was a village brass band, very human, which I called generated because nobody plays that out of tune on purpose. The most convincing «band» of the six was a model. In my defence, she missed one herself, a solo piano piece she was sure was generated, and changed the subject with great dignity.

I am in good company. Last autumn Deezer and Ipsos ran a similar test with about 9,000 people in eight countries, and 97% could not tell the generated tracks from the human one. Deezer also reported that about a third of the tracks delivered to it every day were fully generated. What fooled me fools most people: we listen for imperfection as the sign of a human, and the models have learned to sound clean, while real people, especially at a village festival, do not.

Then, mid-joke, Veronika went serious, which she does without warning. Music is the easy case, she said. The hard one is language: Czech models still stumble on slang, on professional jargon and on Moravian dialect, and nobody notices until a model misreads something that matters. Her team spends its days on exactly that, and it is less glamorous and more useful than generating songs.

My verdict: three out of six means my ears are not a detector, and probably neither are yours. The eight in ten people in that survey who want AI music labelled have a point.
